# Notes
## Abstract
- Potentially the "[[Cosmic shoreline]]" may not hold around M stars due to their harsher XUV environment
- Identify target selection as a combinatorial optimization problem
- Develop statistical framework to test population-level hypotheses from observations
- Combine formation and evolution model, 1D-RCE atmosphere model, and genetic algorithm to simulate populations and find optimal set of observations
- Demonstrate that:
	- If all rocky planets around M stars are indeed bare rocks JWST can efficiently place an upper bound on the atmosphere occurrence rates to less than 1 in 8
	- If the cosmic shoreline hypothesis holds true for M stars, strong evidence can be found within $\sim 500$ observing hours using optimal "wide and shallow" approach
## Main Paper
### Introduction
#### The M-Star Cosmic Shoreline
-  Key objective of JWST is to determine whether and under what conditions terrestrial exoplanets can form and retain atmospheres
	- Observing M stars is particularly useful
	- They are abundant in the solar neighborhood
	- Have smaller stellar radii and cooler stellar temperature improving signal sizes
- Main way to identify if a rocky planet ($<\sim 1.7R_{\oplus}$) has an atmosphere with JWST (secondary eclipse technique)
	- Observe star as planet moves in and out of view behind star with Mid-Infrared Instrument
	- Measure dayside thermal emission of planet (the dayside temperature and energy budget)
	- Expect a thick atmosphere to cool the dayside by redistributing heat
- So far no definitive detection of an atmosphere has been made so can ask if any M-dwarf rocky planets have atmospheres at all
- Study targets at population level can allow for finding more information that is not present in specifics
- Many ways to completely lose atmosphere
	- Thermal escape
	- Loss of high mean molecular volatiles during hydrodynamic escape of primary atmospheres
	- Non-thermal escapes
		- Impact induced stripping 
		- Stellar wind interactions
		- Volatile-poor initial formation
- Many ways to contribute to atmospheric (re-)generation 
	- Outgassing from magma ocean
	- Volcanic replenishment
	- Late-stage delivery of volatiles
- Atmospheric retention
	- Efficient atomic line cooling in the upper atmosphere inhibiting thermal escape
- Radiation environment around M stars is different than more massive stars
	- M stars emit a larger fraction of their bolometric flux in the X-ray and extreme ultraviolet (XUV) range than Sun like stars driving thermal atmospheric escape
	- Have longer pre-main-sequence lifetimes and spin-down so planets are irradiated for much longer
#### Target selection as an optimization problem
- Treat target selection as solving a non-linear [[Knapsack problem]]
	- Each potential observation is an item that incurs a weight in observing time
	- Total weight is the total observing time to be kept under some limit
	- Total value is determined by how well the observations allow for statistically distinguishing different hypotheses
- Non linearity aspects 
	- Total value sin't a sum of the component values but is determined by the synergy among the chosen items
	- Generally diminishing returns to choosing the same object multiple times
- Non linearity behaviors
	- There is no optimal substructure to the problem
		- Optimal solution to large problem doesn't have to have the optimal sub-solution
		- Thus greedy approaches do not work
#### Purpose and structure of this work
- Perform 2 experiments to address 2 questions
	- Should all M-star rocky planets be bare rocks want to quantify what constraints on occurrence rates of atmosphere are achievable
	- Should the M-star Cosmic Shoreline exist want to establish how well the hypothesis can be statistically distinguished
### Methods
- Perform population level injection-recovery simulation
- Process
	- Present 4 parametric hypotheses
	- Start with list of targets smaller than $1.7 R_{\oplus}$ to be observed an consider lists constructed via the emission spectroscopy metric and the priority metric
	- For each target assign an atmospheric composition and surface pressure sampled from a distribution generated by a formation and evolution model
	- Calculate the 1D thermal profile and its emergent secondary eclipse spectrum for each target
	- Obtain observed uncertainties for each target
	- Calculate inferred probability of an atmosphere for each target from its observed flux
	- Use MCMC to estimate associated parameters of each hypothesis and its [[Bayesian information criterion]] 
	- Use a genetic algorithm to perform a discrete optimization to select the best set of targets and numbers of eclipses to maximize the expected difference in BIC between hypotheses
#### Injected hypotheses
- Four possible hypotheses
- **Pessimist hypothesis**
	- No target has an atmosphere
	- Single detection of an atmosphere rejects this hypotheses
	- Use 3 values for how bright the surfaces of all planets are
		1. All planets have blackbody surfaces
		2. Have Bond albedos of 0.2 
		3. Have Bond albedos of 0.4
- **Random hypothesis**
	- Targets have atmospheres at random independent of any specific axes
	- Follow intrinsic binomial probability $p_{int}$ between 0 and 1
- **Cosmic Shoreline hypothesis**
	- Targets without atmospheres and those likely to have atmospheres are separated by a power law in the log-escape speed-bolometric instellation plane
	- Have a wet side where targets *may* have an atmosphere but not necessarily and a dry side where they *cannot*
- **XUV Cosmic Shoreline hypothesis**
	- Same as before but instead of current bolometric flux use cumulative XUV flux to determine if a target can have atmospheres
- Conduct 2 experiments for the 4 hypotheses
	1. Inject Pessimist hypothesis and recover the Random hypothesis
		- Done to place a constraint on the occurrence rate of atmospheres
		- *If planets simply had atmospheres at random $p_{int}$*
	2. Inject Cosmic Shoreline hypotheses and recover the injected Cosmic Shoreline hypothesis and the Random (Or Pessimistic) hypothesis
		- Then find maximum value of $\mathbb{E}[\Delta \mathrm{BIC}]$ between the 2 hypotheses by optimizing for the set of observations
		- Do this for both bolometric and XUV versions
		- Repeat for Pessimist hypothesis as the null hypothesis
#### Best-in-class targets for emission observations
- Construct target lists from the DDT "Targets Under Consideration"
	- Accept values of radius and mass as point values (discussion of ignoring error later on)
	- Rank these targets by metric 
		1. Emission spectroscopy metric (ESM)
		- Define $\mathrm{ESM_{15}}=A \dfrac{B_{\lambda}(T_{max};15\mu m)}{B_{\lambda}(T_{\star};15\mu m)} \dfrac{R^{2}_{p}}{R_{s}^{2}}10^{-m_{K}/5}$
		- $A=7.2\times 10^5$ is a chosen scaling factor
		- $B_{\lambda}(T;\lambda)$ is the Planck function
		- $m_{K}$ is the K-band magnitude
		1. Priority metric
			- $\mathrm{PM}=(4\log_{10}(V_{esc})-\log_{10}(I) +\log_{10}(I_{0}))/\sqrt{ 17 }$
			- $I$ is either the bolometric or the cumulative XUV flux
	- There is little to no overlap between the top 20 targets from either metric
		- $\mathrm{ESM_{15}}$ favors hottest targets
		- Priority Metric favors lowest instellation (coolest targets)
#### Formation and Evolution Model
- From the given injected hypothesis draw a random instance of a population that varies in whether each potential target has an atmosphere or not
- Use PACMAN to obtain a realistic distribution of the atmospheric conditions and total surface pressures of the atmosphere
- Computationally prohibitive to simulate for each target so sample from distribution
#### Atmosphere Model
- Use HELIOS to model thermal structure and emergent flux of the planet
- Use SPHINX model grid to construct stellar spectrum
#### Observation Model
- Use PANDEIA-ENGINE v4.0 to estimate uncertainty in the eclipse depth of a single eclipse for each target
- Assume targets have circular orbits
#### Probability of Atmosphere for Eclipse Observations
- After randomly drawing observation of eclipse depth $d_{i}$ and uncertainty $\sigma_{i}$ for each target assign a probability of having an atmosphere $q_{i}$
- Definition of atmosphere is subjective and fuzzy. Below is their defintion
	- For each target consider 2 mutually exclusive possibilities that the $i$-th planet has an atmosphere $\mathcal{A}_{i}$ and that it is a bare rock $\mathcal{B}_{i}$
	- Define $q_{i}$ as the probability of the former being true given an observation $q_{i}=(\mathcal{A}_{i}|d_{i})$
	- Prior and posterior possibilities sum to 1: $p(\mathcal{A}_{i})+p(\mathcal{B}_{i})=1$ and $p(\mathcal{A}_{i}|d_{i})+p(\mathcal{B}_{i}|d_{i})=1$
	- Can write $q_{i}$ in terms of the Bayes factor $B_{\mathcal{AB},i}$: $q_{i}= \dfrac{B_{\mathcal{AB},i}}{1+B_{\mathcal{AB},i}}$
	- $B_{\mathcal{AB},i}= \dfrac{p(d_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i})}{p({d_{i}|\mathcal{B}_{i}})}= \dfrac{\int p(d_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{A}})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{A}}|\mathcal{A}_{i}d\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{A}})}{\int p(d_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{B}})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{B}}|\mathcal{B}_{i})d\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{B}}}$
		- $\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{A}})$ and $\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{B}})$ indicate the priors on parameters associated with a planet having an atmosphere and being a bare rock
		- Such as surface pressure, gas composition, clouds, bond albedo, or surface mineralogy
		- Use point estimate $B_{\mathcal{AB},i}\approx \dfrac{p(d_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{A}0},\mathcal{A}_{i})}{p(d_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{B}_{0}},\mathcal{B}_{i})}=\dfrac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{shallow},i}}{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bare},i}}$
		- Likelihoods $\mathcal{L}$ for shallow eclipse depth and a bare rock follow the normal distribution: $\ln \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bare},i}=-\dfrac{1}{2}(d_{i}-d_{\mathrm{bare},i})^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{2}+\ln 2\pi\sigma_{i}^{2}$
			- $\sigma$ is the binned error after N eclipses for each target
			- Defined for a single photometric channel
	- Then $q_{i}=p(\mathcal{A}_{i}|d_{i})=\dfrac{1}{1+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bare},i}/\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{shallow},i}}$
		- All inferred $q_{i}$ assume that if a detectable atmosphere exists it looks like 0.1 bar $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ 
#### Population-level statistical inference
- With list of targets and their $q_{i}$s can estimate the parameters associated with each hypothesis
- For full posterior distribution use MCMC
- If only BIC is needed use Powell optimizer 
	- Find parameter values to maximize average BIC for given hypothesis for each realization
	-  the $\Delta \mathrm{BIC}$ is used as the fitness function for the genetic algorithm to optimize the list of targets over
- Important to maximize the difference in BIC between two hypotheses rather than minimizing the BIC of one given hypothesis
	- BIC is only significant in relative terms
	- BIC is suited to select between different models not to select between different samples
#### Target Selection Using a Genetic Algorithm
- Use PyGAD  to use apply a [[Genetic Algorithm]]
	- Each solution is a potential survey that consists of 80 genes that each encode the number of eclipses for each target
	- Use the expected $\mathbb{E}[\Delta \mathrm{BIC}]$ between chosen pair of hypotheses as the fitness function
### Discussion
- Most critical flaw is that optimizing for targets can lead to conclusions that are *merely* statistical
	- Can achieve the strongest possible population level constraints but yield *only* weak individual results
	- To mitigate: can impose a minimum threshold on detection significance
#### Additional Considerations for target prioritization 
- **Host star characterization**
	- Currently only consider stellar effective temperature
	- Beneficial to add information about 
		- Irradiation environment/history
		- Stellar flares during observation
- **Multiplanet systems**
	- Observing targets within multiplanet systems offers best opportunity to conduct a controlled experiment
		- The greatest uncertainty in the planets' birth environment (the evolutionary history of the host star) is shared among multiple planets
	- Also controls for one source of uncertainty in interpreting observations by allowing to compare two planets with the same instrument
- **Unknown or imprecise mass and stale ephemerides**
	- When mass is not directly measured need to rely on mass-radius relationship introducing challenges
		- If a target has a significantly lower density than a truly rocky planet its interpretation in context of atmospheric retention may be challenging
		- Uncertainty in the position of a target relative to the Cosmic Shoreline along the $v_{esc}$ axis
		- Uncertainty in eccentricity and stale ephemeris contributes significantly to uncertainty in the eclipse timing
	- Important to characterize eclipse targets through RV measurements beforehand
#### Robustness of population level inferences
- **Ubiquity of $CO_{2}$**
	- Assume 0.1 bar $CO_{2}$ model as stand-in for all atmosphere models
	- Reasonable assumption as: takes extremely small abundance of $CO_{2}$ to create significant signal 
	- Caveat: atmospheres with compositions or pressures that depart significantly from fiducial model could produce eclipse depths indistinguishable from bare rock
- **Population-level false positives**
	- Possible that systematic effects could lead to false positives/negatives
	- Ensure that target sample spans the full range of escape velocities
		- This provides a leverage in inferring a true 2D trend
## Conclusion
- Developed a fully reproducible, population-level framework for testing whether rocky exoplanets around M dwarfs have atmospheres and whether they follow an empirical "Cosmic Shoreline"
- With planet-formation outputs, 1D radiative-convective modeling, JWST/MIRI secondary eclipse simulations, and genetic optimization simulate population of rock planets around M stars
	- **Occurrence rate constraints**: If all targets were dark bare rocks survey of $\sim 27$ rocky planets would place a 95% upper limit of $\sim 12\%$ on atmosphere occurrence
	- **Evidence for cosmic shoreline:** An optimized set of targets can find strong evidence for the trend
	- **Observational strategy:** Best strategy is a wide and shallow survey of $\sim 50$ targets of which 2-3 eclipses on presumed "dry" planets combined with 4-18 eclipses on "wet" candidates

> Answering the question "do rocky exoplanets around M stars have atmospheres?" is a key science goal of the JWST mission, with 500 hours of Director's Discretionary Time (DDT) awarded to address it. Theoretically, the so-called "Cosmic Shoreline" may not hold around M stars due to their harsher XUV environment, possibly resulting in most rocky planets lacking significant atmospheres -- a hypothesis that remains to be statistically tested through judicious target selection. We identify target selection as a combinatorial optimization problem ("knapsack problem"). We develop a statistical framework to test population-level hypotheses from observations and combine a formation and evolution model, 1D-RCE atmosphere model, and genetic algorithm to simulate populations and find the optimal set of observations. We find that, firstly, if all rocky planets around M stars are indeed bare rocks, JWST can efficiently place an upper bound on the atmosphere occurrence rates to less than 1 in 8, even without optimized target selection, but further improvements to the constraint are cost-prohibitive. Secondly, if the Cosmic Shoreline hypothesis (XUV or bolometric) holds true for M stars, strong evidence ($\Delta$BIC>5) can be found within ~500 observing hours using the optimal strategy of a "wide and shallow" approach. Our statistical framework can be directly applied to upcoming observations to robustly identify the Cosmic Shoreline and to optimize target selection for determining other trends in exoplanet atmosphere observations, including those from future missions.

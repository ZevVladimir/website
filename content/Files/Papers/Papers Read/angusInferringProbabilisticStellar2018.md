# Notes
## Abstract
- Use a [[Gaussian Process Model|GP]] with a [[Quasi-periodic kernel]] allows sampling of posterior probability density function of the periodic parameter
	- Marginalizes over the other kernel hyperparameters using a MCMC approach
- Infer rotation periods from 333 simulated stellar light curves and of 275 *Kepler* stars
## Main Paper
### GP Rotation Period Inference
- To recover stellar rotation period from a light curve using a [[Quasi-periodic kernel|QP]]-[[Gaussian Process Model|GP]] sample the following posterior PDF
	- $y$ light-curve flux data
	- $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ hyperparameters of the kernel described in[[Quasi-periodic kernel|QP]]
	- $\mathcal{L}$ is the QP-GP likelihood function
	- $p(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the prior on the hyperparameters
- Difficulties of sampling the posterior
	- Likelihood evaluation is computationally expensive
	- GP model is very flexible sometimes at the expense of reliable recovery of the period parameter
	- Posterior may often be multimodal
- **Likelihood**
	- For simple Gaussian:
		- $\ln \mathcal{L}=-\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[ \dfrac{(y_{n}-\mu)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{n}+\ln(2\pi\sigma_{n}^{2})} \right]$
			- $y_{n}$ is the data
			- $\mu$ is the mean model
			- $\sigma_{n}$ are the Gaussian uncertainties on the data
		- Written in matrix form: $\ln \mathcal{L}=-\dfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{r}^T\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}\boldsymbol{r}-\dfrac{1}{2}\ln |\boldsymbol{C}| \dfrac{N}{2}\log 2\pi$
			- $\boldsymbol{r}$ is the vector of residuals
			- $\boldsymbol{C}$ is the covariance matrix
	- For GP regression covariance matrix generated by kernel function $\boldsymbol{K}$ replaces $\boldsymbol{C}$
	- Evaluating the likelihood for 10s of thousands of points of a light curve is too slow to scale to many light curves
	- To accelerate
		- Subsample the data
		- Split the light curve into independent sections
- **Priors**
	- Want to avoid large values of $A$ and $\Gamma$ and small values of $l$ to still be able to recover periodic structure that otherwise could not be used in the fit
	- Use ACF to inform the period prior
		- Identify several candidate periods and weight them
- **Sampling**
	- Use MCMC sampler with three different proposals that are weighted
### Performance and Comparison to Literature
- **Simulated Light Curves**
	- Use synthetic lightcurves from Aigrain et al. 2015
- **Method performance**
	- Periods inferred by GP show good agreement with the true periods for the synthetic lightcurves
	- Compute median absolute deviations (MADs) of the results relative to the input periods
	- Also compute the median relative deviation (MRD) as a percentage and the root mean square (rms)
	- Informed prior GP performed better than uninformed prior
- **Comparison with literature methods Synthetic data**
	- ACF
		- Since ACF is non probabilistic the rotation period uncertainties are poorly defined
		- Also requires evenly spaced data and thus not applicable to ground-based light curves
		- Predict outlier periods significantly different than the true period more often than the GP method while otherwise performs as well if not slightly better
	- LS periodogram
		- Formal uncertainty is a noisy estimate of the true uncertainty
		- Performs slightly worse than ACF and GP
		- Also performs better when a filter was applied as described in the text
- **Discussion**
	- Majority of over-predictions are produced when rotation period signal is highly non-sinusoidal
	- QP-GP method provides slightly more accurate rotation periods than ACF or periodogram methods
	- Also produces probabilistically justified uncertainties but they are still underestimated
	- #future-work 
		- Looking at cuts that can be made to remove false positives
			- Applying color cuts to avoid giant contamination
			- Distinctive hyperparameters for variable stars
		- Use an updated, faster solver for calculating GP likelihood
		- Investigate more physically motivated priors
		- Build a noise model for *Kepler* data
			- Since GP models rotation period at same time as systematic noise features you can marginalize over parameters of the noise model
## Conclusion
- Comparing GP, auto-correlation function (ACF), and Lomb-Scargle (LS) periodogram method to recover rotation periods find that the GP method produces the most accurate rotation periods
- GP provides posterior PDF samples that can be used to estimate rotation period uncertainties
- Uncertainties are still underestimated by the GP because it is an approximate model
- GP is only an effective model and not an accurate physical one
	- Captures posterior PDF of periodic component of covariance matrix of a time series
	- Not the actual rotation period of a physical star

>

> Variability in the light curves of spotted, rotating stars is often non-sinusoidal and quasi-periodic – spots move on the stellar surface and have finite lifetimes, causing stellar flux variations to slowly shift in phase. A strictly periodic sinusoid therefore cannot accurately model a rotationally modulated stellar light curve. Physical models of stellar surfaces have many drawbacks preventing effective inference, such as highly degenerate or high-dimensional parameter spaces. In this work, we test an appropriate effective model: a Gaussian Process with a quasi-periodic covariance kernel function. This highly flexible model allows sampling of the posterior probability density function of the periodic parameter, marginalizing over the other kernel hyperparameters using a Markov Chain Monte Carlo approach. To test the effectiveness of this method, we infer rotation periods from 333 simulated stellar light curves, demonstrating that the Gaussian process method produces periods that are more accurate than both a sine-fitting periodogram and an autocorrelation function method. We also demonstrate that it works well on real data, by inferring rotation periods for 275 Kepler stars with previously measured periods. We provide a table of rotation periods for these and many more, altogether 1102 Kepler objects of interest, and their posterior probability density function samples. Because this method delivers posterior probability density functions, it will enable hierarchical studies involving stellar rotation, particularly those involving population modelling, such as inferring stellar ages, obliquities in exoplanet systems, or characterizing star–planet interactions. The code used to implement this method is available online.

As an undergraduate at the University of Maryland (UMD), I have had the opportunity to participate in several research projects. I worked with Dr. Shabnam Jabeen on applying Quantum Machine Learning (QML) to astrophysical problems, Dr. Benedikt Diemer on a machine learning project to better classify dark matter particles in simulations, and with the Students for the Exploration and Development of Space (SEDS) to design, build, and launch custom cube satellite components. These experiences have deepened my passion for astrophysics while equipping me with problem-solving, leadership, and computational skills that I will apply as a Ph.D. student at UMD.

As a freshman at the University of Maryland (UMD), I joined the First Year Innovation and Research Experience (FIRE) program and the Quantum Machine Learning (QML) research stream. With no prior experience in machine learning and quantum computing,  I embraced this challenge as an opportunity to grow and learn. During that summer, I worked with Dr. Shabnam Jabeen, who leads the QML stream, to develop my Python skills and explore concepts such as gradient descent and neural network design. Armed with these core concepts, I worked with five other undergraduates to develop and understand a deep neural network. Over the course of the summer, we learned how to implement and tune the network to analyze Higgs Boson data from the Large Hadron Collider. We investigated and compared activation functions, loss functions, and kernel initializers to optimize our model. Our effort culminated in a poster analyzing the optimal parameters for training a neural network to identify simulated Higgs Boson signal events.

Simultaneously, I began independently exploring quantum circuits and delving into Qiskit, a quantum computing development kit, to grasp the fundamentals of quantum computing. I approached Dr. Jabeen at the start of the fall semester about a project that would connect the new techniques and skills I had been learning to my lifelong passion for astronomy. My inspiration was a paper by Dr. Ana Delgado, who used machine learning to identify which parameters of a dark matter halo are needed to model the galaxy-halo connection. I contacted Dr. Delgado, who kindly provided their code and data and wrote a proposal to compare her method of using classical machine learning to a quantum machine learning model that I would develop under Dr. Jabeen’s guidance.

Over the next year, I trained and tuned a quantum variational regressor model, studied ansatz design, and learned how to implement custom quantum feature maps. Simultaneously, I studied the underlying physics of how dark matter halos influence the formation and evolution of the galaxies that form on them. By the end of this project, I had developed a model capable of making predictions with 70% accuracy, successfully demonstrating the potential of QML for the future of astrophysics. In the spring of 2023, I presented my findings at the Undergraduate Research Day at UMD.

After my first experience with independent research, I was eager to continue gaining experience and developing my research skills. I reached out to Dr. Benedikt Diemer about working on a project that would leverage my skills to learn more about dark matter. He introduced me to a project that focuses on using machine learning to enhance a recently proposed dynamical definition of dark matter halos. The dynamical definition of halos describes a halo as a collection of orbiting particles rather than the traditionally defined spherical overdensity boundaries. The current methods to determine if a particle is orbiting depend on either a computationally intensive but accurate pericenter finder or a fast but relatively inaccurate cut in phase space (like radius and radial velocity space). Dr. Diemer proposed developing a machine learning model that would be trained on a limited but descriptive set of particle parameters to make classifications based on a particle’s characteristics. He required that this method be able to make fast and accurate classifications as well as be interpretable.

After receiving this project, I began researching machine learning models that could meet our objectives. I identified XGBoost, a gradient-boosted decision tree model, as an ideal choice due to its ability to handle datasets containing billions of particles, straightforward implementation, and interpretability. However, to train my model on this large dataset I needed to learn how to handle the memory and computation requirements associated with that. At each step, I faced new problems in areas where I had little to no experience: How do I work with a dataset with billions of data points? How do I parallelize my code with memory constraints? How do I analyze my model’s performance? The first step to answering these questions was to use Zaratan, UMD’s supercomputer, to ensure sufficient memory and computational capability. I learned how to optimize code for high-performance computing and how to navigate the requirements for job submission and management. To enhance my code, I utilized Dask, a parallel computing library that allowed me to process datasets exceeding memory capacity and efficiently distribute the workload across Zaratan.

Additionally, I incorporated GPUs, leveraging their immense speedup capabilities to efficiently train, tune, and adjust my model. After successfully training my model, I evaluated its performance by identifying the types of particles the model tended to misclassify based on their radii and velocities and with the dark matter density profiles it produced. Finally, I investigated the model's decision-making process and the physical insights it provided by using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP values) to determine which features influenced its classifications. Beyond growing as a researcher, I achieved several milestones, including a successful honors defense in November 2024, where I received High Honors, a poster presentation at the Cosmology and Galaxy Astrophysics with Simulations and Machine Learning 2024 Conference hosted by the Simons Foundation in New York City, and the preparation of a first-author paper for submission to the _Astrophysical Journal_.

Finally, I explored astronomy research outside of theory with the Students for the Exploration and Development of Space (SEDS), a student club at UMD. As project lead for one of SEDS’s projects, I led a team of undergraduate students in constructing a cube satellite payload, which included an onboard computer, a radio, and an antenna deployment system—each component designed, built, and assembled by our team. These components will be offered as a cost-effective alternative to off-the-shelf options, providing other university teams with affordable yet equally reliable solutions. Our project concluded with the handing off our payload to the U.S. Naval Academy for a launch into space scheduled for the summer of 2025. Throughout this process, I expanded our team from five to 25 students, organized a successful test launch onboard a high-altitude weather balloon, and developed a team wiki that serves as a knowledge base for low-cost cube satellite construction.

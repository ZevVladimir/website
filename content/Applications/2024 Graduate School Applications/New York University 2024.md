## Personal Statement
“Physics is impossible to understand, I don’t know how you do it”. I’ve heard this countless times, from my first high school physics class to conversations as recently as last week. This preconception has both motivated me to succeed in an area that has been deemed hard but to also work to counter this notion.
I was fortunate enough to grow up in a school district with a robust AP program, where I had the opportunity to take both AP Physics 1 and AP Physics C. Even more impactful than the courses themselves were the two exceptional teachers who inspired and motivated me. Their passion for teaching made physics not only approachable but enjoyable, showing me and my peers that while physics can be challenging, it is also deeply rewarding. This experience sparked my desire to pursue a degree in physics and astronomy at the University of Maryland (UMD), where I could continue discover exciting applications of physics while working to inspire others to explore the wonders of astrophysics.
At UMD, I became increasingly immersed in my classes, driven by a growing passion for learning and a strong desire to engage in research. Attending a large, research-focused institution like UMD provided me with a wealth of opportunities. From enrolling in the First-Year Innovation and Research Experience (FIRE) program to connecting with professors for research projects, I learned how to conduct research and how it can serve as a method to uncover mysteries of our universe but also to share knowledge. Once again, I had excellent mentors who guided me through planning and executing a project. I learned not only how to conduct research but also how to communicate my work effectively and embody the qualities of a strong leader.
Building on what I have learned and experience since those first physics classes I want to continue my journey at New York University ==and work on making physics and astrophysics more accessible to a large and diverse population. Inspired by the exceptional teachers and mentors who have shaped my path, I aim to develop curricula and programs that simplify complex topics and make them approachable. I envision collaborating with NYU or non-profit organizations in New York City to host public events that feature engaging talks on current research and fun, hands-on demonstrations, such as pendulum experiments or interactive activities like building Rube Goldberg machines and bottle rockets. NYU== offers a unique opportunity to achieve these goals through its access to many diverse communities and renowned faculty, enabling me to grow as both a researcher and teacher. NYU’s connection to diverse communities and its distinguished faculty provide an ideal platform for me to grow as both a researcher and educator, enabling me to bridge the gap between science and the public.

## Statement of Purpose
“You’ll have to work much harder.” That was my trombone teacher’s assessment of my readiness for an audition for the New York All-State Band.  I embraced the challenge because of my passion for the trombone and because I saw it as an opportunity to become the best trombonist I could be. I practiced night after night, working slowly through difficult sections of my solo and developing my style. I earned my chair in the All-State Band in my senior year of high school. Playing the trombone cultivated my patience, perseverance, and drive to excel—qualities I will carry into graduate school and, hopefully, into a career as an astrophysicist.

Astrophysics calls for creativity and a willingness to challenge convention, as demonstrated by the scientists who first hypothesized the heliocentric solar system or revealed that only 5% of the energy density of the universe is made up of baryonic matter. As an astrophysicist, I will strive to embody the field's values: originality, teamwork, and outreach. I thrive on unraveling complex concepts in my research and piecing them into clear, effective solutions, exploring new perspectives to tackle problems, and making complicated topics accessible to scientists and nonscientists. Within the field of astrophysics, computational methods provide unparalleled opportunities to push boundaries. I am particularly interested in developing simulations that span a wide range of scales, analyzing their extensive datasets, and comparing them with observations. I believe that with these efforts, we can further refine cosmological parameter constraints and uncover the mechanisms driving the formation and evolution of astrophysical structures, from stars to galaxies. Over the past four years, I have creatively solved research questions, cultivated and employed leadership skills, and developed my teaching and mentoring abilities to contribute to the community. I am confident that my skills and experiences have prepared me to thrive in the astrophysics PhD program at New York University.

As a sophomore, I began my first independent research project at the University of Maryland (UMD), working with Dr. Shabnam Jabeen.  I had spent the previous summer working with her to learn the basics of Quantum Machine Learning (QML), which sparked my curiosity about its potential applications in astrophysics. I wanted to compare the capabilities of QML with classical machine learning (ML) for current astrophysical problems. Exploring the galaxy-halo connection presented a promising test case due to the well-defined relationship between the input parameters and outputs. I reached out to Dr. Ana Delgado, then at Harvard, who had used ML to explore this problem. I also reached out to Dr. Benedikt Diemer at UMD, an expert in dark matter halos, for direct guidance in developing an astrophysical project. Over the next year, I developed a robust comparison method between the QML and ML methods for identifying which characteristics of halos best model the connection between the halos and galaxies. I presented my methods and preliminary results at the Undergraduate Research Day at UMD, and I am excited about the prospect of continuing to use QML as an analysis tool in astrophysics.

While working on this project, Dr. Diemer invited me to join his research group’s weekly meetings, which opened my eyes to the complexities of dark matter halos. I approached Dr. Diemer to undertake a new project with him, and he proposed using ML to enhance the classification of dark matter particles within a simulation, determining whether they belonged to a halo.

With my research into potential machine learning models we decided to use XGBoost. I had found that it was easily implementable, scalable for large datasets, and its decisions were interpretable—qualities essential for achieving our goals. I trained the model using billions of particles from multiple simulations, which quickly overwhelmed my computer. I pivoted to using UMD’s supercomputer, Zaratan, and used specialized libraries to distribute the computational workloads.  Although my model produced very accurate particle classifications, I wanted to dive deeper. I analyzed regions of interest using phase space plots, density profiles, and Shapley values (SHAP) and demonstrated how ML can also be used to gain insight into the astrophysical processes within a halo. This work culminated in my successful senior thesis defense, for which I received high honors. I will submit a first-author paper to the Astrophysical Journal and recently presented a poster at the Cosmology and Galaxy Astrophysics with Simulations and Machine Learning 2024 Conference hosted by the Simons Foundation in New York City.

Alongside my work with theoretical astrophysics, I took on other projects that required me to develop my leadership skills in order to navigate and overcome obstacles, synthesize solutions by incorporating team member input, and foster collaboration. For the past three years, I have led a student team within the Students for the Exploration and Development of Space, where we collaboratively design and construct custom cube satellites. When I was chosen to be the project lead, I  was a sophomore and faced a team that had dwindled to only five members and was on a very tight project deadline. I knew for our mission to be a success, I needed to rebuild the team. Through attending outreach fairs and hosting onboarding events, I soon gathered 25 students who were excited to work together to accomplish our mission. Over the next year, I led my team to our first successful mission consisting of our custom-built computer and radio onboard a high-altitude balloon. Since then, we persevered through many setbacks, from shipping delays to faulty circuits, and successfully handed off our revised payload to the U.S. Naval Academy for an upcoming launch into space next year.

Since high school, I have excelled in and enjoyed teaching as a tutor, teaching assistant, and peer mentor. I started as a tutor for math, English, and writing in my senior year of high school at an online tutoring company. Due to my success in motivating and effectively communicating with my students, I was quickly promoted to manager. I continued to develop my teaching skills when, during the spring semester of my sophomore year, Dr. Jabeen asked if I would be a TA and a peer mentor for her new students working with QML. I happily accepted and for the past two years I have worked with several groups on their projects, developed homework questions, and revised literature reviews. I have also been an astronomy peer mentor to two freshmen this past year. I worked with them to develop their four-year plans, establish study schedules, and provide advice on how to succeed both in the Astronomy program and adjust to college life. Finally, I designed a curriculum for a local Boy Scout troop to teach them about the scale of the universe. Each of these experiences provided me the opportunity to learn how to break down complicated concepts and communicate effectively with diverse audiences.

I am eager to further develop these qualities and my skills as a researcher at New York University. Under Dr. Jeremy Tinker's guidance, I aim to contribute to the development of advanced halo-based galaxy group finders. My prior work with Dr. Diemer, where I created efficient and accurate descriptions of dark matter halos, equips me with the necessary experience. Coupled with my proficiency in Python and C, I am well-prepared to assist in developing robust computational tools for this purpose. Additionally, I am eager to collaborate with Dr. Andrew MacFadyen on research leveraging machine learning and high-performance computing to analyze astrophysical systems. Building on my experiences at UMD, where I honed my expertise in machine learning and supercomputing, I look forward to further refining these skills while contributing to cutting-edge research. I am also committed to giving back to the community by engaging in initiatives like NYU's America Reads and Counts program, where I would tutor students in reading and math, helping them develop confidence in these skills. Furthermore, I am eager to collaborate with peers and faculty to design a program that promotes accessibility and inclusivity in physics and astrophysics, for people of all ages. Overall, I am confident that New York University provides an ideal environment for me to conduct cutting-edge research, engage with a vibrant academic community, and advance my academic and professional development.

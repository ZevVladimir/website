# Essays
## Statement of Purpose
“You’ll have to work much harder.” That was my trombone teacher’s assessment of my readiness for an audition for the New York All-State Band.  I embraced the challenge because of my passion for the trombone and because I saw it as an opportunity to become the best trombonist I could be. I practiced night after night, working slowly through difficult sections of my solo and developing my style. I earned my chair in the All-State Band in my senior year of high school. Playing the trombone cultivated my patience, perseverance, and drive to excel—qualities I will carry into graduate school and, hopefully, into a career as an astrophysicist.

Astrophysics calls for creativity and a willingness to challenge convention, as demonstrated by the scientists who first hypothesized the heliocentric solar system or revealed that only 5% of the energy density of the universe is made up of baryonic matter. As an astrophysicist, I will strive to embody the field's values: originality, teamwork, and outreach. I thrive on unraveling complex concepts in my research and piecing them into clear, effective solutions, exploring new perspectives to tackle problems, and making complicated topics accessible to scientists and nonscientists. I desire a career in astrophysics research because it combines all of these qualities. Over the past four years, I have creatively solved research questions, cultivated and employed leadership skills, and developed my teaching and mentoring abilities to contribute to the community. I am confident that my skills and experiences would allow me to thrive in the astrophysics PhD program at the University of Wisconsin-Madison.

I began my first independent research project at the University of Maryland (UMD) as a sophomore, working with Dr. Shabnam Jabeen.  I had spent the previous summer working with her to learn the basics of Quantum Machine Learning (QML) and sparking my curiosity about its potential applications in astrophysics. I wanted to compare the capabilities of QML with classical machine learning (ML) for current astrophysical problems. Exploring the galaxy-halo connection presented a promising test case because of the clear connection between the input parameters and outputs. I reached out to Dr. Ana Delgado, then at Harvard, who had used ML to explore this problem. I also reached out to Dr. Benedikt Diemer at UMD, an expert in dark matter halos, for direct guidance in developing my project. Over the next year, I developed a robust comparison method between the QML and ML methods for identifying which characteristics of halos best model the connection between the halos and galaxies. I presented my methods and preliminary results at the Undergraduate Research Day at UMD,  and I am excited about the prospect of using QML as an analysis tool in astrophysics.

While working on this project, Dr. Diemer invited me to join his research group’s weekly meetings, which opened my eyes to the complexities of dark matter halos. I approached Dr. Diemer to undertake a new project with him, and he proposed using ML to enhance the classification of dark matter particles within a simulation, determining whether they belonged to a halo.

With my research into potential machine learning models we decided to use XGBoost as I found it was easily implementable, scalable for large datasets, and its decisions were interpretable. I trained the model using billions of particles from multiple simulations, which quickly overwhelmed my computer. I pivoted to using UMD’s supercomputer, Zaratan, and used specialized libraries to distribute the computational workloads.  Although my model produced very accurate particle classifications, I wanted to dive deeper. I analyzed regions of interest using phase space plots, density profiles, and Shapley values (SHAP) and demonstrated how ML can also be used to gain insight into the astrophysical processes within a halo. This work culminated in my successful senior thesis defense, for which I received high honors.  I will submit a first-author paper to the Astrophysical Journal and will present a poster at the Cosmology and Galaxy Astrophysics with Simulations and Machine Learning 2024 Conference hosted by the Simons Foundation in New York City.

Alongside my work on QML, I took on other projects that required me to develop my leadership skills in order to navigate and overcome obstacles, synthesize solutions by incorporating team member input, and foster collaboration. For the past three years, I have led a student team within the Students for the Exploration and Development of Space, where we collaboratively design and construct custom cube satellites. When I was chosen to be the project lead, I  was a sophomore and faced a team that had dwindled to only five members and was on a very tight project deadline. I knew for our mission to be a success, I needed to build a team. Through attending outreach fairs and hosting onboarding events, I soon gathered 25 students who were excited to work together to accomplish our mission. Over the next year, I led my team to synthesize our ideas and skills, resulting in the successful completion of our first mission with a high-altitude balloon equipped with a custom-built computer and radio. Since then, we persevered through many setbacks, from shipping delays to faulty circuits, and successfully handed off our payload to the U.S. Naval Academy for an upcoming launch into space next year.

Since high school, I have excelled in and enjoyed teaching as a tutor, teaching assistant, and peer mentor. I started as a tutor for math, English, and writing in my senior year of high school at an online tutoring company. Due to my success in motivating and effectively communicating with my students, I was quickly promoted to manager. I continued to develop my teaching skills when, during the spring semester of my sophomore year, Dr. Jabeen asked if I would be a TA and a peer mentor for her new students working with QML. I happily accepted and worked with several groups on their projects, developed homework questions, and revised literature reviews. I have also been an astronomy peer mentor to two freshmen this past year. I worked with them to develop their four-year plans, establish study schedules, and provide advice on how to succeed both in the Astronomy program and as they adjust to college life. Finally, I designed a curriculum for a local Boy Scout troop to teach them about the scale of the universe. Each of these experiences provided me the opportunity to learn how to break down complicated concepts and communicate effectively with diverse audiences.

I am eager to further develop my skills as a researcher at the University of Wisconsin-Madison. My strong interest and background in machine learning align closely with the work of Dr. Moritz Münchmeyer, particularly in developing machine learning methods to explore fundamental physics. Additionally, I am excited for the opportunity to work with Dr. Elena D’Onghia with their research on the applications of numerical simulations to understand our Milky Way. I am also interested in working with Dr. Christy Tremonti and Dr. Matthew Bershady to use galaxy surveys to probe fundamental properties of our universe. Beyond research, I am committed to giving back to the community and am particularly drawn to the University of Wisconsin’s Space Place program. This initiative offers a wonderful opportunity to share my passion for space, connect with the local community, and learn from expert presenters. Overall, I am confident that the University of Wisconsin-Madison provides an ideal environment for me to conduct cutting-edge research, engage with a vibrant academic community, and advance my academic and professional development.

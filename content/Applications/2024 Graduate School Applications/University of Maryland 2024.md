“You’ll have to work much harder.” That was my trombone teacher’s assessment of my readiness for an audition for the New York All-State Band.  I embraced the challenge because of my passion for the trombone and because I saw it as an opportunity to become the best trombonist I could be. I practiced night after night, working slowly through difficult sections of my solo and developing my style. I earned my chair in the All-State Band in my senior year of high school. Playing the trombone cultivated my patience, perseverance, and drive to excel—qualities I will carry into graduate school and, hopefully, into a career as an astrophysicist.

Astrophysics calls for creativity and a willingness to challenge convention, as demonstrated by the scientists who first hypothesized the heliocentric solar system or revealed that only 5% of the energy density of the universe is made up of baryonic matter. As an astrophysicist, I will strive to embody the field's values: originality, teamwork, and outreach. I thrive on unraveling complex concepts in my research and piecing them into clear, effective solutions, exploring new perspectives to tackle problems, and making complicated topics accessible to scientists and nonscientists. ==Within the field of astrophysics, computational methods provide unparalleled opportunities to push boundaries. I am particularly interested in developing simulations that span a wide range of scales, analyzing their extensive datasets, and comparing them with observations. I believe that with these efforts we can further refine cosmological parameter constraints and uncover the mechanisms driving the formation and evolution of astrophysical structures, from stars to galaxies.== Over the past four years, I have creatively solved research questions, cultivated and employed leadership skills, and developed my teaching and mentoring abilities to contribute to the community. I am confident that my skills and experiences would allow me to thrive in the astrophysics PhD program at the University of Maryland.

I began my first independent research project at the University of Maryland (UMD) as a sophomore, working with Dr. Shabnam Jabeen.  I had spent the previous summer working with her to learn the basics of Quantum Machine Learning (QML) which sparked my curiosity about its potential applications in astrophysics. I wanted to compare the capabilities of QML with classical machine learning (ML) for current astrophysical problems. Exploring the galaxy-halo connection presented a promising test case because of the clear connection between the input parameters and outputs. I reached out to Dr. Ana Delgado, then at Harvard, who had used ML to explore this problem. I also reached out to Dr. Benedikt Diemer at UMD, an expert in dark matter halos, for direct guidance in developing an astrophysical project. Over the next year, I developed a robust comparison method between the QML and ML methods for identifying which characteristics of halos best model the connection between the halos and galaxies. I presented my methods and preliminary results at the Undergraduate Research Day at UMD, and I am excited about the prospect of using QML as an analysis tool in astrophysics.

While working on this project, Dr. Diemer invited me to join his research group’s weekly meetings, which opened my eyes to the complexities of dark matter halos. I approached Dr. Diemer to undertake a new project with him, and he proposed using ML to enhance the classification of dark matter particles within a simulation, determining whether they belonged to a halo.

With my research into potential machine learning models we decided to use XGBoost. I had found that it was easily implementable, scalable for large datasets, and its decisions were interpretable—qualities essential for achieving our goals. I trained the model using billions of particles from multiple simulations, which quickly overwhelmed my computer. I pivoted to using UMD’s supercomputer, Zaratan, and used specialized libraries to distribute the computational workloads.  Although my model produced very accurate particle classifications, I wanted to dive deeper. I analyzed regions of interest using phase space plots, density profiles, and Shapley values (SHAP) and demonstrated how ML can also be used to gain insight into the astrophysical processes within a halo. This work culminated in my successful senior thesis defense, for which I received high honors. I will submit a first-author paper to the Astrophysical Journal and will present a poster at the Cosmology and Galaxy Astrophysics with Simulations and Machine Learning 2024 Conference hosted by the Simons Foundation in New York City.

Alongside my work with theoretical astrophysics, I took on other projects that required me to develop my leadership skills in order to navigate and overcome obstacles, synthesize solutions by incorporating team member input, and foster collaboration. For the past three years, I have led a student team within the Students for the Exploration and Development of Space, where we collaboratively design and construct custom cube satellites. When I was chosen to be the project lead, I  was a sophomore and faced a team that had dwindled to only five members and was on a very tight project deadline. I knew for our mission to be a success, I needed to build a team. Through attending outreach fairs and hosting onboarding events, I soon gathered 25 students who were excited to work together to accomplish our mission. Over the next year, I led my team to our first successful mission consisting of our custom-built computer and radio onboard a high-altitude balloon. Since then, we persevered through many setbacks, from shipping delays to faulty circuits, and successfully handed off our revised payload to the U.S. Naval Academy for an upcoming launch into space next year.

Since high school, I have excelled in and enjoyed teaching as a tutor, teaching assistant, and peer mentor. I started as a tutor for math, English, and writing in my senior year of high school at an online tutoring company. Due to my success in motivating and effectively communicating with my students, I was quickly promoted to manager. I continued to develop my teaching skills when, during the spring semester of my sophomore year, Dr. Jabeen asked if I would be a TA and a peer mentor for her new students working with QML. I happily accepted and worked with several groups on their projects, developed homework questions, and revised literature reviews. I have also been an astronomy peer mentor to two freshmen this past year. I worked with them to develop their four-year plans, establish study schedules, and provide advice on how to succeed both in the Astronomy program and as they adjust to college life. Finally, I designed a curriculum for a local Boy Scout troop to teach them about the scale of the universe. Each of these experiences provided me the opportunity to learn how to break down complicated concepts and communicate effectively with diverse audiences.

I am eager to further develop these qualities and my skills as a researcher at UMD. Working with Dr. Massimo Ricotti, I could develop simulations to further investigate the formation of the first stars and galaxies using my skills in C for the development of these simulations and in Python for the analysis of our results. I would also be interested in continuing my work with Dr. Diemer with their research on dark matter halos as well as new projects investigating the impact of different dark matter and gravity models on galaxy evolution. I am particularly interested in continuing to leverage the exceptional computing resources, such as Zaratan, and the expertise of the strong faculty on campus to support this research. Finally, I want to participate in programs like the observatory open houses and work with my peers and the faculty at UMD to spread interest and love for astronomy. Overall, I am confident that UMD provides an ideal environment for me to conduct cutting-edge research, engage with a vibrant academic community, and advance my academic and professional development.

Within the field of astrophysics, I am particularly interested in using computational methods to develop simulations that span a wide range of scales, analyze their outputs, and compare them with observations. I believe that with these efforts we can refine cosmological parameter constraints and deepen our understanding of the mechanisms driving the formation and evolution of astrophysical structures. Over the past four years, I have solved research questions, strengthened my leadership skills, and refined my mentoring abilities, which I am confident have prepared me to excel in the astrophysics PhD program at Harvard University.

As a sophomore, I began my first independent research project with Dr. Shabnam Jabeen at the University of Maryland (UMD) as part of the First-year Innovation and Research Experience’s Quantum Machine Learning (QML) stream.  I chose to compare QML with machine learning (ML) in exploring the galaxy-halo connection as there is a clear connection between the input parameters and outputs. I reached out to Dr. Ana Delgado, then at Harvard, who had previously used ML to explore this problem. I also contacted Dr. Benedikt Diemer at UMD, an expert in dark matter halos, for direct guidance in developing this astrophysical project. Over the next year, I developed a robust comparison method to identify which characteristics of halos most effectively model the connection between halos and galaxies. I presented my findings at UMD's Undergraduate Research Day and look forward to continuing to use QML as an astrophysical analysis tool.

With my research into potential machine learning models we decided to use XGBoost. I had found it easy to implement, scalable for large datasets, and interpretable—qualities essential for our goals. I trained the model using billions of particles from multiple simulations, which quickly overwhelmed my computer. I pivoted to using UMD’s supercomputer, Zaratan, and used specialized libraries to distribute the computational workloads.  Although my model produced very accurate particle classifications, I wanted to dive deeper. I analyzed regions of interest using phase space plots, density profiles, and Shapley values (SHAP). With these techniques I demonstrated how ML can also be used to gain insight into the astrophysical processes within a halo. This work culminated in my successful senior thesis defense, for which I received high honors. I will submit a first-author paper to the Astrophysical Journal and recently presented a poster at the Cosmology and Galaxy Astrophysics with Simulations and Machine Learning 2024 Conference hosted by the Simons Foundation.

## Research Description
Since November 2022 I have worked with Dr. Benedikt Diemer to train a machine learning model that refines a dynamical definition of dark matter halos. Unlike traditional methods using a fixed radius, this definition defines the halos by the particles that are orbiting within the halo’s potential. Machine learning was chosen to identify orbiting particles due to its ability to process complex information efficiently, delivering fast and accurate classifications with a smaller dataset. Our model achieved 97% accuracy, reproduced the density profiles, and accurately classified particles in highly dense areas of the model’s phase space. From May 2022 to December 2023 I worked with Dr. Shabnam Jabeen to apply different quantum machine learning models on astrophysical datasets. With Qiskit’s Variational Quantum Regressor we compared its performance to classical machine learning in identifying the importance of different halo parameters in the galaxy-halo connection. I also applied a quantum random forest to compare its results with those from my work with Dr. Benedikt Diemer. While QML showed promise, it struggled with large datasets, resulting in lower accuracy.

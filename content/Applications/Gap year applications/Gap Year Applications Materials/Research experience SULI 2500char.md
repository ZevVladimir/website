I began my first independent research project at the University of Maryland (UMD) as a sophomore, working with Dr. Shabnam Jabeen.  I had spent the previous summer working with her to learn the basics of Quantum Machine Learning (QML) which sparked my curiosity about its potential applications in astrophysics. I wanted to compare the capabilities of QML with classical machine learning (ML) for current astrophysical problems. Exploring the galaxy-halo connection presented a promising test case because of the clear connection between the input parameters and outputs. I reached out to Dr. Ana Delgado, then at Harvard, who had used ML to explore this problem and to Dr. Benedikt Diemer, an astronomy professor at UMD who is an expert in dark matter halos, for direct guidance in developing an astrophysical project. Over the next year, I developed a robust comparison method between the QML and ML methods for identifying which characteristics of halos best model the connection between the halos and galaxies. I presented my methods and preliminary results at the Undergraduate Research Day at UMD, and I am excited about the prospect of using QML as an analysis tool in astrophysics.

For my next research project, I approached Dr. Diemer, and he proposed using ML to enhance the classification of dark matter particles within a simulation, determining whether they belonged to a halo.

With my research into potential machine learning models we decided to use XGBoost. I had found that it was easily implementable, scalable for large datasets, and its decisions were interpretable—qualities essential for achieving our goals. I trained the model using billions of particles from multiple simulations, which quickly overwhelmed my computer. I pivoted to using UMD’s supercomputer, Zaratan, and used specialized libraries to distribute the computational workloads.  Although my model produced very accurate particle classifications, I wanted to dive deeper. I analyzed regions of interest using phase space plots, density profiles, and Shapley values (SHAP) and demonstrated how ML can also be used to gain insight into the astrophysical processes within a halo. This work culminated in my successful senior thesis defense, for which I received high honors. I will submit a first-author paper to the Astrophysical Journal and I presented a poster at the Cosmology and Galaxy Astrophysics with Simulations and Machine Learning 2024 Conference hosted by the Simons Foundation in New York City.
